Quote fast, follow up

How do I stop driving to quotes that were never going to buy?

A Home Service Edge guide · by Eric Lenhardt

Guides Prompts

Ask three questions before you agree to drive out: the price range, what the job is and what part of town it's in, and the timeline. A real customer answers all three in about a minute, and the person who was never going to book sorts themselves out before you've burned half a day on the road. Then spend two minutes having a free AI assistant rewrite those three questions in your own words, so they sound like you and not like a call script. For the leads who never phone you at all, a free Google Form asks the same three questions and routes the out-of-area ones to a polite no before they ever land on your calendar.

That's the answer. Here's what a dead quote trip actually costs, the card to copy, and the prompt that puts it in your voice.

What a dead quote trip actually costs you

A quote trip is unpaid work until the job books, and most operators are running several of them for every job that lands. That's the leak. It isn't bad quoting; it's the driving you did to write a quote that was never going to turn into money.

"A quote trip is unpaid until it books." - Eric, in the video

You drive across town, you walk the job, you write it up, you send it. You only get that time back if they say yes. In the video I put it in plain numbers rather than a survey figure, because the honest version is the one you can check against your own week:

"For a lot of businesses, it's three or four trips for every job that actually books." - Eric, in the video

You'll notice there's no dollar total on this page and no percentage. That's deliberate. There's no clean, sourceable figure for what tire-kickers cost a home-service business, and a fake-precise number would be its own small hype. So do the arithmetic with your own numbers instead: your round-trip drive time, times what an hour of your time is actually worth, times the trips this month that were never going to book. That's your leak. And the bigger your service area, the worse it gets, because the drive is the expensive part.

Asking three questions isn't rude. It's respect.

The reason most operators never fix this has nothing to do with software. It's that asking questions on the phone feels rude, like you're interrogating a stranger or turning away work you can't afford to turn away. Flip that around.

"Three questions is respect. It respects the customer's time and it respects yours." - Eric, in the video

Think about who each answer actually protects. A serious customer with a real job is happy to answer three plain questions; it takes them a minute and it saves them an afternoon of having you out for a number they were never going to accept. The person who was only ever price-shopping does the sorting for you:

"A serious customer answers them happily. A tire-kicker talks themselves out of the trip for you." - Eric, in the video

You're not being picky and you're not turning down real work. You're just not giving up a Saturday to quote a job that was never in your range or your area.

The three questions (copy this card)

This is the whole fix, and it fits on an index card. Three questions, asked every time, before you commit to the drive. Copy it exactly as it is, swap in your own numbers and your own service area, and tape it where you take calls.

Before you drive out, ask:

  1. Range: "Most jobs like this land between $___ and $___, depending on what we find. Does that range work for what you had in mind?"
  2. Scope + area: "Tell me a little about what you need done, and what part of town are you in?"
  3. Timeline: "When are you hoping to get this taken care of? Is this a now thing, or are you still planning?"

Optional: "Are you getting a few quotes on this, or are we your first call?"

Each one is doing a single job.

Range is the one that sorts a ready customer from someone fishing for the cheapest number in town. You are not quoting the job on the phone. You're checking you're in the same ballpark before you drive to it. In the video I use four hundred to eight hundred dollars as the example; your numbers will be different, and the shape of the sentence is what matters.

Scope and area is two questions in one. It confirms this is work you actually do, and it confirms the job is inside the area you'll drive to.

"The 'what part of town' is the one that kills the forty minutes drive." - Eric, in the video

Timeline separates a job with a date on it from "someday, just curious." Somebody planning a project for next spring is a real person and maybe a real customer later, but they are not this week's quote trip.

The optional fourth earns its place in some trades and not others. Ask it if knowing you're one of five bids changes how you'd price or whether you'd go. The first three do most of the work.

One more free upgrade while you're at it: save the three questions as a phone shortcut so you can fire them at a text or a web lead in one tap. On iPhone that's Settings → General → Keyboard → Text Replacement; on Android it's Gboard → Settings → Dictionary → Personal dictionary. Type a short code, it expands to all three. Get the three questions written in your own words

The card above is written in my voice, and that's exactly the problem with it. Reading somebody else's lines off an index card is how a phone call starts sounding like a call center.

"If you read somebody else's words off a card, it's gonna sound like a script, and customers can hear that." - Eric, in the video

So don't read mine. Use mine as the shape, and spend two minutes getting the same three questions written the way you actually talk. Open a free AI assistant (ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini, any of them works on the free tier; in the video I run it in Claude), paste the prompt below, and fill in the four brackets: your trade, your city, what your typical jobs run, and the areas you cover. Paste in one real message you've actually sent a customer where it asks, because that sample is what makes the output sound like you instead of like the internet.

I run a [your trade] business in [your city or area]. Write three short, friendly qualifying questions I can ask on the phone before I drive out to give a quote: one about budget range, one about what the job is and where it is, one about timing. My typical jobs run between $[low] and $[high]. I only serve [your areas]. Keep the questions in my voice, plain and warm, never pushy. Here is a message I actually sent a customer, so you can match how I talk: [paste one of your texts]. Then write two polite one-line replies I can use when a job is outside my range or outside my service area, so I can say no kindly.

Every part of that prompt is doing a job. Three short, friendly questions keeps it from writing you a sales script. My typical jobs run between is what makes the range question specific instead of generic. Keep the questions in my voice plus your pasted text is why it comes back sounding like you. And the last sentence is the part people skip and then wish they hadn't:

"It also writes the part nobody likes doing. The two polite lines for saying no." - Eric, in the video

Those two decline lines are worth as much as the questions. "That one's a bit outside our service area" is short, kind, and finished. Once you have a sentence you're comfortable saying, turning down the wrong job stops feeling harsh, and you stop taking the trip just to avoid an awkward moment on the phone.

Then do the one thing that isn't optional: read what it gives you and cut anything you'd never actually say. You're the one who has to say these out loud to a stranger. The writing gets help; the talking stays yours.

For the leads who never call: a free Google Form that qualifies for you

If your leads come in through your website or Facebook instead of the phone, a free Google Form can ask the same three questions and turn away the ones that don't fit before they ever reach you. Google Forms is free with any Google account, and this is a setting, not a project. It's the routing setting I show on screen in the video.

Build the three questions as Multiple choice or Dropdown answers (a budget range, a list of the areas you cover, a timeline). Then use Google's own routing option, which Google's help page calls "Go to section based on answer", to send each answer somewhere. A good answer moves the lead forward to a section that collects their name, address, and phone. A bad one, an area you don't serve or a range that isn't close, goes to a short section that says something like "Thanks for asking. That one's outside what we cover." Google's help page also spells out the piece that makes it a real gate: "If you want the survey to end based on an answer, you can also choose Submit form." That's the setting that stops an out-of-area lead from ever becoming a trip on your calendar.

One catch that trips people up: Google Forms does not email you a new response by default. Submissions collect in the form's Responses tab and in any spreadsheet you link. If you want a ping when a real lead comes through, open the Responses tab, click the ⋮ menu, and switch on "Get email notifications for new responses".

"Costs nothing, and it runs by itself." - Eric, in the video

(Google Forms routing re-verified against Google's own help page on 2026-08-18. "Go to section based on answer" is available on Multiple choice and Dropdown questions only.)

Copy my form instead of building one

If you would rather not build it yourself, take mine. It is the same three questions, already wired up with the routing, and it is free: no email, no account of mine, no gate. Out of your area, or over your range, and it sends a polite decline by itself. It never lands on your plate as a trip.

The form template (free, make your own copy): https://docs.google.com/forms/d/1h7_fCE37SBfj1zF0saTDkDKN92fA-DyLVliaf9jUahw/copy

One thing to know before you click, so it is not a surprise: you need to be signed into a Google account, the same as any Google Form. Copying it drops your own copy in your own Drive, and nothing you do touches mine. Building your own from scratch needs a Google account too.

Make it yours in about four minutes:

  1. Open the copy link. Google asks "Make a copy?" Say yes. The copy is yours, in your Drive, and nothing you do touches the original.
  2. Change the three [Your area 1/2/3] options to the parts of town you actually drive to. Add or delete options as needed. Leave "Somewhere else" alone, that one is the filter.
  3. Change $[low] and $[high] to your real range. Your numbers, not anyone else's.
  4. Read the decline message in the last section and put it in your own words.
  5. Change the form title from "Request a Quote" to your business name if you want.
  6. Hit Send, copy the link, and put it on your website and your Facebook page as the "Get a quote" button.

Optional: turn on email notifications for new responses under the Responses tab so a real lead pings your phone.

Rather not copy a file from someone you don't know? Build your own instead. The section above walks through it, and Google's own help page covers the routing setting.

If you already run a CRM, build the questions into your request form

Same idea, different place: if you pay for a field-service CRM, put the three questions into the intake form your leads already fill out, so every lead qualifies itself before it reaches you. I don't show a CRM on screen in the video on purpose, because most of the operators watching don't run one and nobody should buy software to ask three questions. But if you already have it, use it.

  • Jobber calls this Requests, and its help page states plainly: "Requests are available on all plans." You build the form under Gear Icon → Settings → Requests and Bookings → Forms → Add New Form. It's a drag-and-drop builder, so you add a budget-range dropdown, a "what do you need done" field, a service-area question, and a timeline, and mark whichever ones you want as required. Every submission comes into Jobber as a request with the person attached as a lead. (Jobber, Request Basics)
  • Housecall Pro has a Lead Form you can embed on your site. Go to Settings, then pick Leads from the left menu, and toggle on the fields you want, including Service Details, "How did you hear about us?", Frequency, and one custom question. One honest catch straight from their help page: "Currently, any fields added to your Lead Form will be required." So keep it short, or every question you add becomes mandatory for the customer. Housecall Pro's page doesn't say which plan the Lead Form is on, so check it against your own account rather than taking my word for it. (Housecall Pro, Lead Form)

Three questions. About a minute on the phone. The difference between a booked job and a wasted afternoon.

What's next (the upgrade)

Once the three questions are a habit, and only once they are, the upgrade is letting them run without you: the free Google Form on your quote page, or the request form inside the CRM you already pay for. Same three questions, asked automatically, on the leads that come in while you're under a sink.

What you can skip for now is the paid version of this. You'll see ads for AI receptionists that answer every call around the clock and qualify every lead for you. In the video I say what I actually think about them:

"But you don't need one to ask three questions before you drive out. Start with the card. It's free." - Eric, in the video

The full line in the video puts a price on the category: "you'll see ads for AI receptionists that qualify every lead for you, some of them two hundred to five hundred dollars a month. For a bigger business, those can be real tools." That's an honest read, not a takedown. If you're missing more calls than you can physically answer, a service like that solves a real problem. But it isn't the fix for this one. Get the habit working on your own calls first, and let the paid version earn its way in later.

One honest limit, so nobody sells you a fairy tale: a card and a form don't replace the judgment call. They just make sure the question gets asked before you commit the drive. You're still the one who decides whether the job is worth doing. That's the whole point, though. You decide before you're standing in someone's driveway, not after.

FAQ

Isn't it rude to ask about budget on the first call?

No, it's the opposite. You're not asking for their bank balance; you're giving them a price range and asking whether it works, which saves them the time of having you out for a number they were never going to accept. Most customers are relieved that someone is being straight with them.

What if I scare off a real customer by qualifying them?

A real customer with a real job answers three plain questions without blinking. The only people these questions push away are the ones who were never going to book, which is exactly who you want them to push away, before you've spent the gas and the afternoon.

Do I need software to do this?

No. The whole fix is three questions you can tape to the wall, and all three assets on this page are free, with nothing of mine to sign up for. The form needs a free Google account, the same as any Google Form; the card and the prompt need nothing at all. A Google Form or a CRM request form just makes sure the questions get asked on web leads too. The card by the phone works on its own for nothing.

What do I say when the answer is no?

Have one short line ready and use it every time. "That one's a bit outside our service area" is enough. The prompt above writes two of these for you in your own words, which is the point of it. Having the sentence ready is what stops you taking a bad trip just to avoid an awkward moment.

How is this different from following up on a quote I already sent?

This is screening before you drive out and quote. Following up is about the quotes you already gave that went quiet. They're two different leaks: qualify on the front end so you make fewer wasted trips, then follow up on the back end so the quotes you did give don't die in silence.
